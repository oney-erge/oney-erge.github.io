Variants of speculative decoding

Part 1 used the simplest form of speculative decoding: a separate small model drafts a fixed number of tokens, and the large model checks them in one pass. Most variants change one of three choices: where the draft comes from, how many tokens to draft, and whether to check one guess or several. This part gives an overview, with my measurements for two of them.

Oney ErgeOctober 14, 20266 min read

Every variant keeps the final step of Part 1: the large model, the target model, checks the draft and keeps only the tokens it agrees with. With greedy decoding, the output is therefore the target model's own output in every variant. What changes is how the draft is made, and so how often it is right and what it costs.

The sections below take the three choices in order.

Where the draft comes from

A separate draft model

A separate small model is the original form [1]. It needs no training, but it must share the target model's tokenizer, and it takes memory: about 1.3 GB of GPU memory on my laptop (Part 1).

The target model's own layers

Self-speculation drafts with the target model itself, using only some of its layers, so no second model is stored. Draft & Verify skips selected middle layers while drafting and needs no training. LayerSkip trains the model so that its early layers can already predict the next token, then drafts from those layers [2].

I tested the untrained form on Qwen3-14B, drafting from the output of its first 4 or first 8 of 40 decoder layers. In this short test, with one prompt and 4 tokens, the target model accepted none of the draft tokens at either depth, so every round produced exactly one token [8]. Without training for early exit, the early layers of this model do not predict the next token well enough.

Extra prediction heads

Medusa adds several small output heads to the target model, each predicting a token further ahead, and trains only those heads. EAGLE trains one small extra layer that predicts the target model's internal representation one step ahead, and drafts from it [3]. Both need a training run for each model, but no separate draft model.

Text already in the prompt

Prompt lookup decoding needs no draft model at all. When the last few tokens of the answer also appear in the input prompt, it proposes the tokens that followed them there [4]. This works well when the answer repeats the input, as in summaries or code edits. Lookahead decoding also has no draft model: the target model generates and checks short candidate sequences in parallel during its own passes [4].

How many tokens to draft

Fixed speculation drafts the same 8 tokens in every round. When the draft goes wrong early, the rest of it is wasted. Adaptive methods stop drafting when the next draft token is likely to be rejected: SpecDec++ trains a small head that predicts the chance of a rejection, and DISCO uses a small classifier [5]. Both report modest gains over a fixed length, about 7 to 10%.

In streamed inference, the trade-off is lopsided. Eq. (2) of Part 1 shows why: a wasted draft token costs tens of milliseconds, while one pass of the target model costs about 20 seconds. Stopping early saves little, and stopping too early gives up tokens that the pass could have checked almost for free.

My measurements agree. I tested three adaptive policies on Qwen3-14B against a fixed draft length [8]:

  1. Policy 1A learning policy that adjusts k after each round cut k to 1 after a few rejections and was slower than the fixed draft: 7.7 against 7.2 s per token, on one prompt with 16 sampled tokens.
  2. Policy 2A threshold policy tied with the fixed draft, within the run-to-run noise of the same test.
  3. Policy 3A stopping policy that weighs the chance of rejection against the cost of drafting was slower on the four prompts of Part 1: 9.8 against 9.2 s per token.

For a model streamed through a small GPU, a long fixed draft is close to the best choice. Adaptive length matters more when the target model fits on the GPU and a pass is fast.

One guess or a tree of guesses

A chain of draft tokens has one weak point: a single wrong token discards everything after it. A tree draft keeps several alternatives at each position, and the target model checks the whole tree in one pass, with each token seeing only its own branch [6]. The longest branch the target model agrees with is kept. Figure 1 compares the two with a simple example.

SpecInfer introduced tree checking for serving LLMs, and Medusa and EAGLE also check trees [6]. Trees suit streamed inference especially well. When a pass is dominated by loading weights, checking hundreds of tokens costs about the same as checking one. SpecExec uses this for models offloaded to RAM: it builds large trees from the draft model's most likely continuations and reports up to 20 accepted tokens per pass [6].

Conclusions

The main variants of speculative decoding were compared by where the draft comes from, how many tokens are drafted, and whether one chain or a tree is checked, and two of the choices were measured on my laptop. The following were concluded:

  1. Every variant keeps the target model's check, so the variants differ in speed and cost, not in the output, apart from relaxed acceptance rules such as Medusa's.
  2. In a short test, drafting from the target model's own early layers did not work without training: the target model accepted none of the draft tokens from the first 4 or 8 layers of Qwen3-14B.
  3. Adaptive draft length did not beat a fixed 8-token draft in my measurements, because in streamed inference a wasted draft token costs little next to one pass.
  4. Tree drafts, which I have not measured yet, suit streamed inference, because one pass can check many tokens for about the cost of one; SpecExec reports up to 20 accepted tokens per pass.

Therefore, for a large model streamed through a small GPU, the simplest form, a separate draft model with a long fixed draft, was the best of the options I tested, and tree drafts are the next variant to test.
