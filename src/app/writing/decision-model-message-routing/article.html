How a Decision Model Routes Agent Messages on a CPU

My local agent asks an 8B LLM what every message is and waits about 15 s for the answer. A 0.85B decision model answered the same question on the laptop's CPU in half a second, about as accurately. This note shows the test on 141 messages, and where the small model falls short.

Oney ErgeOctober 29, 20264 min read

I run YBM, an agent on my laptop that I message on Telegram [7]. Before it can act, it must decide what a message is: chat, a question about its own work, or a task, and for a task, which of its tools to use. YBM calls these 15 choices routes.

Today an LLM makes that call: Qwen3-VL 8B [6], which fills the laptop's 8 GB GPU and runs a third of its layers on the CPU. It takes about 15 s per message, before YBM even replies "on it", and it reports a confidence of 0.95 to 1.00 whether it is right or wrong, so YBM cannot tell a sure answer from a guess.

With the GPU full, and swapping models in and out slower than the answer itself, anything faster has to run on the CPU, in under 2 s per message.

A model that picks an option instead of writing text

A decision model reads a text and a question with a fixed list of options, then returns a probability for each option in one pass, without generating any words [1]. Routing is that kind of question: here are 15 routes with one-line descriptions; which one fits this message?

The probability helps only if it is honest. A well-calibrated model is right about 90% of the time when it says 90% [4], so an agent can act on sure answers and ask the LLM about the rest.

The main family I tested is d3 from the vLLM Semantic Router project, from 0.59B to 4.5B parameters, under the Apache-2.0 license [1]. llama.cpp has served these models since October 12, 2026 [2], so no Python model code runs. I also tested LiquidAI's d1 models [3] and five other open models. On the CPU the biggest speed gain came from quantization, storing each weight in about 4 bits instead of 8: it halved the time at the same accuracy.

Two sets of messages

I wrote 98 messages that cover all 15 routes, from "hi" to "rename the photos in Pictures by date", and labeled each one. Because I also wrote the route descriptions while looking at these messages, they may flatter every model.

So I scored the finalists on 43 messages I had not written for this test: YBM's end-to-end test catalogue. Their labels follow a fixed rule, the tools each test requires, and were committed before any model saw them.

I counted the share of messages sent to the right route, the real tasks answered as chat (the mistake that makes an agent silently do nothing), and the median time per message on 8 threads of a Ryzen 9 5900HS. I repeated the runs of the leading models; d3-lite gave identical answers in all three of its runs.

The results

Only d3-lite was both under my limit and as accurate as the rest: 93% of the 98 messages in 0.5 s (Figure 1). The LLM got 92% in 15 s, and its confidence stayed between 0.95 and 1.00 for right and wrong answers alike.

Table 1 adds the held-out messages. They were harder for every model: long requests with several steps. There the LLM was slightly ahead, 88% against 84% for d3-lite: two messages out of 43, within the noise of so small a set. Every d3-lite miss on these messages came with a probability of 0.87 or less. Its probabilities are not always honest, though: it sent "can you read PDFs?" to the document tools at 98%.

Using both models

The two models make different mistakes: on the 98 messages, d3-lite got 5 right that the LLM missed, and the LLM got 4 right that d3-lite missed. YBM now has one setting with four choices: the LLM alone; the decision model recorded beside the LLM for comparison; the decision model alone on clear task routes; or the decision model first on every message it is at least 90% sure of, with the LLM taking the rest and still writing chat replies.

Replayed on the recorded answers and times, the last choice kept the LLM's accuracy on both sets, while the decision model settled 69 of the 141 messages alone, 2 of them wrongly, in 0.6 s instead of 15 to 17 s. A message that arrives while a task is running always goes to the LLM, because it may be a correction to that task.

On the real Telegram bot, the decision model routed 4 of 11 messages alone in under a second, and 6 of 7 end-to-end test tasks passed. The one failure was a message the LLM had routed.

Kept running, d3-lite holds about 1 GB of RAM and 2% of one CPU core. With the 8B model generating text without pause, d3-lite's median time rose to 1.1 s and the 8B slowed by a fifth. That is a worst case: in normal use there is one decision per message.

The point

On this laptop a 0.85B decision model routes a message in half a second on the CPU, as accurately as the 8B LLM on my own messages and slightly less on harder ones, and its probabilities show when it is unsure. In front of the LLM, it settled about half the messages alone without lowering accuracy. It does not replace the LLM; it lets the agent skip the slow call when the answer is clear.
