How H6.5 Optimizes Weight Placement

H6.5 chooses where to keep model weights so the GPU spends less time waiting for them. The weights themselves remain unchanged.

Oney ErgeOctober 14, 20265 min read1 min video

Llama-3.3-70B needs about 141 GB for its BF16 weights. My paper tests how to place them across storage, RAM, and GPU memory on a 32 GB RTX 5090 [1]. The implementation and results are in Afterimage [2].

What H6.5 optimizes

Each weight tensor, such as an attention matrix, can stay compressed or uncompressed on the SSD, unpacked in RAM, or unpacked on the GPU. H6.5 searches for a placement that reduces request time within fixed memory budgets. The model still computes its layers in order.

The main experiment allows 8 GB of GPU memory, including 5.2 GB for persistent weights, and 16 GB of RAM. With 723 tensors, testing every possible placement live is impractical.

Why timing matters

A data-movement rule favors tensors that save the most transfer bytes per byte of memory. Offloading systems such as FlexGen and Accelerate also consider memory capacity and data movement [3] [4]. H6.5 considers when those transfers occur: loading that overlaps computation may add no delay, while a late load makes the GPU wait (Figure 1).

In a recorded Llama request, the data-movement placement left the 2.1 GB output head compressed on the SSD. Reading and unpacking it after the last decoder layer made the GPU wait 1.56 s (Figure 2).

H6.5 evaluates whole placements rather than always pinning the output head. In laptop tests, it kept the head in RAM and rearranged attention and feed-forward tensors instead.

How the search works

H6.5 records reads, unpacking, copies, and computation in real requests, including which events must finish before others can start. It then estimates alternative placements by substituting measured preparation times and replaying those dependencies:

cj=dj+maxk∈pred(j)ck,T^=maxjcj (1)

Each event finishes at cj: its duration dj plus the latest finish among its predecessors, pred(j). The last event determines the predicted request time T̂.

The search improves several feasible starting placements step by step, using a replay score that penalizes unstable predictions. The selected placement is frozen before live tests on new requests.

Preparing this Llama placement took about 9 minutes, including 3.4 s to score 1,817 placements. This is a setup cost, not an extra cost per request. Preparation is repeated when changes to the model, hardware, workload, or budget require a new placement.

What the placement changed

H6.5 changed the placement of 198 of 723 tensors, freeing GPU space for the output head (Figure 3). Both plans are predicted to read about 78 GB from storage per pass; their timing differs.

Keeping the output head ready avoids its temporary GPU allocation, helping explain the lower peak memory. Across three repeat studies on new prompts (35 rounds), pooled request time was about 18% lower than traffic and 15% lower than greedy. Memory was lower in every round; latency gains varied by session.

Limits

The evaluation covers two machines, mainly with one-token requests. Replay estimates guide the search; Table 1 reports live measurements.

In an exploratory 32-token Qwen3-14B test, the one-token placement was slower than streaming all weights from the SSD. A follow-up placement with no host-resident weights was faster on new prompts, consistent with preserving more operating-system file cache. Replay does not model this cache.
